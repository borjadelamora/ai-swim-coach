# Breath control & underwater work — CO2 tolerance, breakouts, dolphin kick

Reference for the Physiologist and Coach when the session touches breath restriction,
breath-holds, breakouts, or underwater kick. GENERIC method — swimmer specifics live in
memory/ and win on conflict.

## What actually limits a breath-hold
- The urge to breathe is driven by CO2 ACCUMULATION (hypercapnia), not low oxygen. A
  swimmer who "can't hold it" almost always has untrained CO2 tolerance, not a small
  gas tank. This is why the quality trains fast (days-weeks): much of the gain is the
  brain recalibrating an alarm, plus better mechanics.
- Under work, CO2 production scales with effort — the same hold that is easy fresh is
  brutal after a hard block. Fatigued holds are a DIFFERENT, harder quality than calm
  holds, and must be trained deliberately, not assumed to transfer.
- Mechanics are half the battle: a clean streamline and an efficient kick lower the
  oxygen cost of the same distance. A degrading breakout under fatigue is often economy
  collapsing, not willpower.

## The two trainable states
1. CALM/FRESH control — holding a pattern relaxed at low-moderate effort. Cheap to
   train, low risk, builds the tolerance floor and the skill. Belongs early in a
   session or as recovery-adjacent work.
2. UNDER-FATIGUE control — holding the pattern late, on accumulated work. The expensive,
   race-relevant quality. Dose it like hard work: engineered pre-fatigue first, the
   controlled exposure after, more rest than plain equivalents, quality monitored per
   rep. State (1) work is the build path toward state (2) — progress the state as one
   of the dials, not both states maximally in one session.

## Tools, lightest to heaviest
- br-N patterns (breathe every N strokes) INSIDE reps: br3 → br5 → br7 → br9. Moderate
  effort first; the pattern is the load.
- Breath-count rest (nB) BETWEEN reps: doubles as rest control and CO2 exposure —
  shrinking nB across rounds is a density dial.
- Breath budgets per rep/length ("≤2 breaths per length"): self-paced restriction that
  scales naturally with rep distance.
- Breakout standards: fixed underwater kick count off EVERY wall, or a fixed breakout
  distance/marker; the standard (not the clock) is the goal on task sets.
- Underwater distance ladders: breakout distance grows across reps at easy effort —
  skill + tolerance without pace pressure.
- Dynamic CO2 density: rounds of moderate reps where REST shrinks while the breathing
  pattern holds — exposure through density, never through maximal single holds.

## Dosing rules
- Breath restriction is an OVERLAY on the day's energy-system work and adds a hidden
  cost the pace clock doesn't show: a restricted rep ALWAYS needs more rest than its
  plain equivalent — never carry a plain send-off across unchanged.
- Progress ONE dial: pattern (br3→br5) OR pace OR rep length OR rest density. Stacking
  maximal restriction + tight rest + fatigue on a first exposure produces failed holds
  and teaches panic, not tolerance.
- Frequency beats heroics: repeated controlled exposure across sessions outperforms
  occasional maximal efforts — and keeps risk near zero.
- Expect fast early gains, then a plateau: when a pattern is comfortable two sessions
  running, advance a dial; don't ride the comfortable version for weeks.
- Heat adds hidden cost (outdoor summer pools): breath-restricted work in heat runs
  harder than the clock shows; when in doubt, add rest.

## Measurement — make it countable
Breath work hides from the clock, so give it counts: breaths taken per rep, kicks held
per wall on the LAST tired round, breakout distance vs a fixed feature, pattern held vs
broken per rep. One countable standard per dedicated session keeps the quality's
progress testable instead of a feeling.

## Safety — hard rules (non-negotiable, especially training alone)
- NEVER hyperventilate before a hold or a restricted rep — it delays the CO2 alarm
  without adding oxygen and is the direct path to shallow-water blackout.
- No maximal static breath-holds when training alone. All solo breath work is DYNAMIC
  and submaximal: restriction with an escape hatch, never "hold as long as possible."
- The turn-approach breath is always allowed regardless of pattern position — safety
  and turn quality outrank the count.
- Dizziness, tingling, tunnel vision, or a metallic taste = the block ends now, breathe
  freely, swim easy. No exceptions, no "one more rep."
These are design constraints, not lecture material — encode them in the set (breath
budgets, escape hatches), don't bolt warnings onto every session.

## Underwater dolphin kick (the breakout vehicle)
- The kick comes from the hips and core, transmitted through loose ankles; a locked
  streamline (arms squeezing the head, body one line) matters more than kick amplitude.
  Small-fast beats big-slow underwater.
- Both directions propel: the up-kick is half the cycle — a down-only kick wastes the
  rebound. Cue tempo before amplitude; rushing shrinks the kick.
- Timing off the wall: glide the push-off's peak speed first (a beat of pure streamline),
  start kicking as speed falls toward swim speed, surface before stalling. Kicking too
  early fights the glide; surfacing too late trades speed for distance.
- Under fatigue the HOLD degrades before the kick mechanics — protect a minimum standard
  (e.g. 2 kicks every wall) rather than chasing maximum distance on fresh walls only.
